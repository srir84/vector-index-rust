vindex · benchmark results

Fastest on 9 of 13 datasets at full size

vindex is a search index for AI embeddings, the lists of numbers that stand for texts, photos or words. Given a question, it finds the 10 closest items without checking them all. Here it races the fastest rivals on 13 public datasets of 187,843 to 1.45 million items. It is level with the fastest rival on 2 (Yandex, GIST1M) and behind on 2 (GloVe, GloVe-100).

All on one Apple-silicon Mac, which favours vindex: read the box below before trusting the numbers. These are the final version’s numbers (part 5); an earlier version’s (part 4) are shown beside.

Read this before trusting the numbers

  • The machine favours vindex. vindex has hand-written vector code for Apple chips (NEON) and was built with profile-guided optimization. Most rivals were written for Intel and AMD chips first. HNSW (hnswlib) has no vector code for Apple chips at all, so its numbers here say little: it is shown, but does not count as a rival. Faiss and LoRANN use their own Apple-chip code. Glass’s own Apple-chip code is switched off upstream, so it runs plain loops that the compiler vectorizes. RoarGraph’s Intel code runs through a translation layer, and ScaNN in a Linux container. On an Intel or AMD server the results may differ.
  • Not every strong index was tried. SymphonyQG needs Intel’s AVX-512, and NGT-QG’s fast search code runs on Intel and AMD chips only. Faiss and LoRANN were added after the code was frozen. “Fastest rival” means the fastest of Glass, ScaNN, RoarGraph, CoreNN, Faiss (HNSW, HNSW-SQ8, IVF-PQ) and LoRANN.
  • vindex learns from sample questions drawn from the same source as the test questions, for its search effort and its extra links. RoarGraph uses them too, and LoRANN on the three chatbot datasets; the others do not.
  • Held back for one round each. The index was built in five parts. Each held-back dataset was kept out of one part, then used like the others; only GloVe-100 stayed locked until the code was frozen.
  • Rerun it yourself: the rivals’ scripts, versions, patches and every result are in vector-index-experiments, and ours in vector-index-rust.

Dataset by dataset

  • LAION, 1 million web photos, searched with text: we are 2.08× as fast as Faiss IVF-PQ, the fastest rival here.
  • Llama, 2nd head, 256,921 lookups from another part of Llama 3: we are 1.95× as fast as RoarGraph, the fastest rival here.
  • Yi, 187,843 lookups inside the Yi language model: we are 1.82× as fast as RoarGraph, the fastest rival here.
  • Llama, 256,921 lookups inside the Llama 3 language model as it reads a novel: we are 1.75× as fast as RoarGraph, the fastest rival here.
  • GooAQ, 1.45 million answers from Google's “people also ask” boxes: we are 1.49× as fast as Glass, the fastest rival here.
  • NYTimes, 268,761 New York Times articles, as word counts: we are 1.43× as fast as Glass, the fastest rival here.
  • ImageNet, 1.28 million photos, searched with text captions: we are 1.38× as fast as Faiss IVF-PQ, the fastest rival here.
  • arXiv, 1.32 million summaries of science papers: we are 1.36× as fast as Glass, the fastest rival here.
  • Landmark, 739,757 photos of famous places: we are 1.34× as fast as Glass, the fastest rival here. Glass’s quickest setting already finds 96.72%, so its speed is that setting’s.
  • Yandex, 1 million photos, searched with text: level with Glass (1.08× its speed).
  • GIST1M, 979,000 photos, described by hand-made features: level with LoRANN (1.01× its speed).
  • GloVe, 1.17 million words from Twitter: ScaNN is 1.36× as fast as we are.
  • GloVe-100, 1.16 million words from Twitter, from a second word model: ScaNN is 1.37× as fast as we are.

In part 4: On GIST1M, the dataset CoreNN's author tested on, our index answers 17.1x as many questions per second as CoreNN at 95% of the true top 10 (one core), and uses 76% less memory with the full numbers on SSD (9% less with them in memory too).

What we did not beat, and other limits

  • Not beaten: on GloVe, ScaNN is 1.36× as fast as we are; on GloVe-100, ScaNN is 1.37× as fast as we are.
  • Level, not ahead: Yandex (1.08× Glass’s speed); GIST1M (1.01× LoRANN’s speed). Within 10% counts as level: timing the same pair again has moved a ratio by up to 16%.
  • Memory: our speeds are with everything in memory, where we use 0.92 to 2.13× the fastest rival’s memory per item. With the full numbers left on the SSD we use 1.7 to 6.5× less than that, but answer 2 to 23× fewer questions per second.
  • The bucket search was left out: on GloVe and GloVe-100, ScaNN groups the items into buckets and scans the closest buckets’ short codes instead of walking links. We built that way of searching too, but at full size on GloVe it did not beat ScaNN, so it is not part of the index.
  • Which rivals: at full size we ran only the rivals that were fastest on each dataset in the 100,000-item samples, or within 1.3× of the fastest (ScaNN on NYTimes), plus HNSW everywhere and CoreNN on GIST1M. That list was fixed before any full-size run. Their runs are part 4’s; only GloVe-100’s rivals (ScaNN, Glass and HNSW) ran in part 5. DiskANN, slower in the samples, was not rerun. SymphonyQG needs Intel’s AVX-512 and cannot run on this Mac. After part 5’s code was frozen, Faiss (HNSW, HNSW-SQ8 and IVF-PQ) and LoRANN ran on all 13 datasets, and from then on HNSW (hnswlib) does not count as a rival: it has no vector code for Apple chips, and Faiss HNSW stands in. Picking rivals on the samples missed some: Faiss and LoRANN, run everywhere, beat the sample-picked rival on LAION, ImageNet and GIST1M. ScaNN, a bucket index like Faiss IVF-PQ, ran at full size on three datasets only.
  • Their settings: each rival tried 9 settings on the tuning questions (ScaNN 6) and kept its fastest at 95%. Their authors may know better ones. Many picks sit at the edge of the grid (Glass and RoarGraph at the most links tried, Faiss IVF-PQ at the larger bucket count), so a wider grid might make them faster. CoreNN ran on its author’s own settings only. Faiss HNSW reused HNSW’s settings, and Faiss HNSW-SQ8 Glass’s (HNSW’s where Glass did not run). Faiss IVF-PQ tried 2 bucket counts and LoRANN 2–4 settings, with and without its reduced size. LoRANN got the sample questions only on the three chatbot datasets, as its documentation advises for questions unlike the items; on the three text-to-photo datasets, where our index turned its question links on, it did not.
  • Containers and patches: ScaNN has no Mac version, so it ran in a Linux container on the same Mac (8 cores), which may cost it some speed. Glass, RoarGraph and CoreNN needed small changes to build on a Mac; their search code was not changed, except that RoarGraph’s Intel-only distance code runs through a translation layer (SIMDe), which may be slower than code written for Apple chips.
  • Fresh builds are steadier, not fully steady: in part 4 a fresh build could be up to 11.3% faster or slower than the last, because the index’s setting search, which times trial builds, kept the faster of two close choices by any margin. Now ties go to the plainer choice, and close calls are timed longer. Three fresh builds of each sample made the same picks on 11 of 12 datasets (before: 5), and those were within 1.0% of each other. ImageNet’s sample still flips: one choice sits right on the bar. At full size, the frozen run’s Yandex build picked differently from three builds made just before it, and is 8.3% slower than the slowest of them.
  • A busy machine: a macOS storage scan, not ours, used up to 4.3 other cores during our runs. Speeds are on one core, where this mattered little in earlier checks.

How the race was kept fair

  • Every index ran on the same Mac: Apple M3 Max, 16 cores, 128 GB of memory, macOS 27.0.1.
  • For each test question we know the true 10 closest items, found by checking every item. An index finds 95% when, on average, 9.5 of its 10 answers are among them.
  • Every index picked its settings on 1,000 tuning questions. The speeds here are on 1,000 other test questions, read off at exactly 95%, the middle of 5 timed runs.
  • Our code and the rivals’ builds were frozen, with a fingerprint of every file, before the full-size runs, in part 4 and again in part 5. GloVe-100 stayed locked until then.
  • Before part 5’s changes we wrote down what we expected. Each guess is marked right or wrong below.

Adding, deleting and updating items

Each index was built on 90% of its items, then 10% more were added, 10% deleted and 10% updated, in one mixed order. Its accuracy was then compared with a fresh build of the final items. The rule: drift under 1 point, and no deleted or outdated item ever returned. None ever came back.
